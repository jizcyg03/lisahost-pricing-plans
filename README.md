LisaHost's price list starts at **¥35/month** for a plain CN2 GIA box and at **¥68/month** for anything with a residential IP attached. Annual specials go as low as **¥199/year**, and the ceiling is **¥1,899/month** for an unlimited-traffic residential line in Japan. Between those two ends sit roughly sixty plans across the US, Hong Kong, Singapore, Taiwan, Japan, Korea, the UK, Germany and Vietnam, and the price differences between them are rarely about CPU or RAM.

If you only want the short answer: the ¥68 tier is the entry point, but four different products share it, and picking the wrong one is the most common way people waste money here.

## The four things LisaHost sells under the word "residential"

This matters more than any table, because the price gap between ¥68 and ¥169 is not a markup on the same product.

**Native IP.** The IP is registered in the same country as the machine. It is still a data-centre range, and platforms can see that. This is what the ¥199/year and ¥299/year US 9929 plans are.

**Dual-ISP residential IP.** The IP's ownership record shows a local consumer ISP, so risk-scoring tools read the connection as a household rather than a rack. This is the ¥68–¥158 monthly 9929/4837/New York/Chicago line-up, and most of the country plans.

**Home broadband VDS.** The IP comes off an actual home connection from an operator such as IIJ in Japan, Astound in Seattle/LA, or T-Mobile and Frontier in California. This is the cleanest tier and the most expensive, starting at ¥169/month and reaching ¥1,899/month unlimited.

**NAT shared IP.** One IP shared across customers with a handful of forwarded ports. LisaHost lists it around ¥599–¥3,999/month. Unless you specifically need a few ports on a residential IP, skip it; an independent IP at ¥68 does more.

A dual-ISP IP and a real home-broadband IP are not interchangeable, and the price difference is the honest signal. Buyers who assume "residential" means the same thing everywhere are the ones who leave one-star reviews.

## The arithmetic that decides most purchases

**The ¥68 tier is four different products.** On 9929 you get 50Mbps and 1TB. On 4837, New York or Chicago you get 300Mbps and 3TB. In Singapore or the UK you get 300Mbps and 6TB. Same price, twelve times the traffic if you pick Singapore. The trade is routing: 9929 is the premium China-optimised path, and Singapore is explicitly not China-direct, so you would be relaying through Hong Kong or Japan anyway. If the workload is "keep a US residential presence cheaply", 9929 Lite is the right buy. If it is "move data", it is the wrong one.

**Unlimited costs more and delivers less speed.** The 9929 Unlimited Lite is ¥498/month for a 20Mbps pipe. The ¥68 Lite gives you 50Mbps. You pay ¥430 extra every month to remove a 1TB cap and lose more than half your bandwidth. That is a fair trade only for something that runs continuously at low throughput, such as a polling bot or a long-lived session box. For anything with bursts, buy the fixed-traffic plan and watch the counter.

**Annual billing is roughly a 40% discount, not a 90% one.** ¥499/year works out at ¥41.6/month against ¥88 for the comparable monthly 9929 Basic, but the annual box has 600GB of monthly traffic instead of 2,000GB. You are buying a smaller machine for longer. ¥399/year for 4837 is ¥33.3/month, again with 600GB. Read the annual column as "a cheaper, thinner plan", not "the same plan at a discount".

**CERA is the one place where the official page shows its own discount.** The Lite plan is listed at ¥40 against a ¥55 list price, and Basic at ¥50 against ¥75. That is 27% and 33%, printed on the product page rather than inferred.

## Limits, refunds, and who should walk away

**Refund policy splits by product line.** The US 9929, 4837, New York, Chicago, Singapore and Hong Kong plans carry a 48-hour no-questions refund. The home-broadband VDS lines (Seattle, LA, Taiwan unlimited VDS, Japan IIJ, Germany residential VDS) are marked as special products: refunds go to site balance only, not back to your card or Alipay. The ¥2 CERA trial and the CERA trial generally are non-refundable. If a cash refund matters to you, do not buy a VDS.

**Abuse enforcement is stricter on residential IPs than on ordinary VPS.** Bulk mail, scanning, phishing, and resource abuse are grounds for suspension without refund, because a single complaint can cost the provider the whole IP block. This is not unique to LisaHost, but it is the reason a residential box is a bad choice for anything grey.

**Some regions are not China-direct.** Singapore, Taiwan and the UK pages say so outright. If your work depends on a low-latency path from mainland China, budget for a relay through Hong Kong or Japan, or buy the 9929 or Hong Kong lines instead.

**Traffic allowances are tight on the premium routes.** 1TB on the ¥68 9929 plan disappears quickly with video. The generous allowances sit on the cheaper routes (Singapore 6TB, UK 6TB, 4837 3TB), which is the trade the pricing is built around.

**Public reputation is thin, not proven either way.** The Trustpilot page for the brand holds a single review, so it says nothing about overall satisfaction. Most write-ups that rank well are affiliate pages, and at least some state they never rented a test machine. Treat the marketing copy and the sceptical forum posts as equally unverified, and use the 48-hour window or the ¥2 trial to check the IP yourself.

**Who should not buy here:** anyone who mainly needs raw compute, storage or unmetered bandwidth for media serving. A plain data-centre VPS is cheaper per core and per terabyte. The residential premium only pays off when the IP attribute is the product.
